# The Beetle That Read The Galaxy

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Celestial Navigator

## The hook
Under a moonless African sky, *Scarabaeus satyrus* rolls its dung ball in a dead-straight line by locking onto the Milky Way itself — the band's light-polarization gradient serves as a stellar compass. Dacke et al. (*Current Biology* 23, 2013: 298–300) showed experimentally that with the galactic band blacked out in a planetarium the beetle's path collapses from straight to random, making it the first invertebrate confirmed to steer by a galaxy.

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait)
- **Palette:**
  - `#0A0B14` — moonless night sky (deep indigo-black substrate)
  - `#1C2340` — upper atmospheric indigo
  - `#E8DDB5` — Milky Way band ivory (real diffuse-galactic-light tint)
  - `#D4A574` — warm brass (navigator's instrument / highlight)
  - `#8A8F99` — cool chart gray (grid, type secondary)
- **Mood:** quiet, exacting, cosmological — a nautical star-chart that happens to carry an insect.
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A diagonal Milky Way band crossing a dark starfield from upper-right to lower-left, rendered as a soft directional gradient with scattered pin-point stars.
  - The dung beetle in profile at the lower edge of the chart, rolling a dark sphere, placed on a dashed heading line that extends from a horizon mark up through the Milky Way.
  - A navigator's azimuth protractor at the top-left corner, with the beetle's bearing locked onto the Milky Way's major axis — the one compositional idea that fuses *insect* and *galaxy*.
- **Motion:** the dashed heading line draws forward (dash-offset ease-out) from the beetle through the galactic band, the Milky Way band glow swells once at the moment the line locks, and the azimuth needle swings to lock on the galactic axis. 5-second loop.
