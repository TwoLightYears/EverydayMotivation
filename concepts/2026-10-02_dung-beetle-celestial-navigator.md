# The Beetle Who Navigates by the Galaxy

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Celestial Navigator

## The hook
On moonless nights the African dung beetle *Scarabaeus satyrus* rolls its ball along a dead-straight escape path by orienting to the diffuse band of the Milky Way itself — when researchers fitted beetles with tiny cardboard caps that blocked only the galactic band (leaving individual stars visible) their trajectories collapsed into aimless spirals, making it the first non-human animal shown to navigate by the galaxy (Dacke, Baird, Byrne, Scholtz & Warrant, *Current Biology* 23 (2013): 298–300).

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#060915` — night ink (sky vacuum)
  - `#141A33` — deep indigo (upper atmosphere, Milky Way dark lanes)
  - `#AEBCE3` — galactic haze (pale blue dust)
  - `#F0E5B6` — star cream (bright stars, galactic core highlight)
  - `#C78A4A` — scarab bronze (dung ball, horizon rim, accent)
- **Mood:** reverent, exacting, nocturnal cartographic
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A diagonal band of Milky Way — bright galactic core, dust lanes, scattered star field — anchoring the upper 2/3 of the composition.
  - Silhouette profile of a scarab beetle standing on its forelegs, pushing a dung ball along a thin horizon line.
  - A single bearing line drawn from the beetle's path up through the galactic core — a surveyor's sight-line that fuses the role (navigator) with the hook (galaxy-as-compass).
  - Compass tick marks and a bearing readout rendered as a draftsman's survey annotation; type block in the lower third.
- **Motion:** stars twinkle on a slow stochastic schedule; the Milky Way band drifts in with a long spring (as if the camera is rising past the horizon); the bearing line scribes itself out along the galactic axis; the beetle inches a few pixels forward, dung ball turning. One 5-second loop.
