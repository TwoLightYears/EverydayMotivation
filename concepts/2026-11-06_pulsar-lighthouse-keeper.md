# The Millisecond Lighthouse Keeper

**Pairing:** Pulsar as Lighthouse Keeper

## The hook
A pulsar is a city-sized neutron star whose magnetic beams sweep past Earth like a rotating lighthouse lamp; PSR B1937+21 — the first millisecond pulsar ever found — fires one pulse every 1.5578 milliseconds (642 flashes per second) and keeps that cadence to better than one part in 10¹⁴, a timing stability that rivals the best atomic clocks on Earth (Backer & Kulkarni et al., *Nature* 300, 1982).

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#060818` — deep-space ink (backdrop, negative space)
  - `#141A35` — interstellar twilight (framed drafting board)
  - `#7FA5FF` — ionized beam blue (the synchrotron radiation beam)
  - `#EEF3FF` — hot neutron-star white (the star itself, pulse peaks)
  - `#F06C4A` — bow-shock orange (accent markers, timing ticks)
- **Mood:** precise, implacable, cosmically calm
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A tiny bright neutron star at the composition's optical centre, offset slightly left of the drafting frame's midline.
  - Two narrow magnetic-axis beams emerging through the star, tilted from the rotation axis — the lighthouse lamp.
  - Concentric wavefronts rippling outward at each pulse, like the pencil-strokes of a timing chart.
  - A timing strip along the right margin — pulse ticks at exactly 1.558 ms spacing — plus a clean callout dial reading **T = 1.5578 ms**.
  - A faint celestial grid underneath the whole piece — the keeper's chart table.
- **Motion:** the beam pair rotates steadily about the rotation axis (one full revolution ≈ 4 s for legibility, not literal); wavefronts expand outward on each pass; the timing-strip ticks light in sync — one primary spring-eased sweep with a pulse flash at each alignment.
