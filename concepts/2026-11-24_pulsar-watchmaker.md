# The millisecond metronome

**Pairing:** Pulsar as Watchmaker

## The hook

A millisecond pulsar is a neutron star ~20 km across that spins hundreds of times per second — PSR B1937+21 rotates at 641.9 Hz — and its rotation is so stable that pulsar-timing arrays track the arrival of its radio pulses to better than 100 nanoseconds per year, a long-term precision that rivals the best atomic clocks. By watching for coordinated sub-microsecond deviations across a galaxy-sized network of these clocks, astronomers have started to detect the gravitational-wave hum of the universe.

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait)
- **Palette:**
  - `#05070F` — deep space ink
  - `#0E1430` — nebula midnight
  - `#4A1A86` — violet synchrotron haze (soft)
  - `#E24AC5` — hot magenta core (bright)
  - `#FFCFEA` — pale pink beam rim
  - `#FFD766` — gold tick accent (the clockwork)
- **Mood:** Precise, cosmic, horological.
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A pulsar rendered as a brilliant magenta/white point at the pivot of a watch dial.
  - Two opposing lighthouse beams (the magnetic-pole axis) crossing the dial, one brighter (toward viewer) and one dimmer (away).
  - A ring of watchmaker tick marks around the beam — major ticks at the twelve hours, finer subdivisions between — explicitly fusing neutron-star geometry with a watch face.
  - A pulse-arrival time strip below the dial: evenly spaced gold ticks on a horizontal axis, labeled `Δt = 1.558 ms`.
- **Motion:** The two beams rotate about the pulsar at a constant angular velocity — one complete 360° revolution over roughly 4 s, eased with a spring on entrance. In sync, each time a beam crosses the top of the dial, a new gold tick lands on the pulse-arrival strip, cementing that beam = tick = metronome.
