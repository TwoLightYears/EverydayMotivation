# The Beetle Who Reads The Galaxy

**Pairing:** African Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Astronomer

## The hook

On moonless nights, *Scarabaeus satyrus* rolls its dung ball in a straight line by orienting to the diffuse light band of the Milky Way — the first non-human animal shown to navigate by the galaxy itself. Dacke et al. (*Current Biology* 23, 2013, 298–300) placed beetles under a planetarium sky and confirmed that removing the stars destroyed the straight-line heading; leaving only the galactic band restored it.

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080×1350 portrait.
- **Palette:**
  - `#07080F` — deep night sky
  - `#141A2E` — midnight blue haze
  - `#E6DEC0` — Milky Way band (star-cream)
  - `#C79A57` — dung-ball warm ochre
  - `#7C88A8` — pale twilight steel (annotations/rules)
- **Mood:** cosmic, deliberate, quietly reverent.
- **Key visual elements:**
  - The Milky Way as a soft diagonal band of stellar light bisecting the sky.
  - A single small beetle silhouette in profile, ball ahead of it, on a low horizon.
  - A dashed sight-line rising from the beetle to align exactly with the galactic band's axis — one composition that *argues* the pairing: the tiny insect measuring the sky.
  - Astronomer's-ledger annotations in the margin: azimuth reading, galactic-plane label, small compass rose.
- **Motion:** stars twinkle at low amplitude while the beetle's sight-line draws upward in one spring-eased stroke and locks onto the Milky Way axis; the dung ball rolls forward the smallest amount to settle into its heading — the hook made visible in a single 5-second beat.
