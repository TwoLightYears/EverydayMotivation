# Walking by Starlight

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Astronomer

## The hook
Rolling its ball at night, the South African dung beetle *Scarabaeus satyrus* walks a dead-straight line by using the Milky Way itself as a compass — the first invertebrate ever shown to orient by starlight. In planetarium tests, the beetles held bearing with only the diffuse band of the galaxy visible, but spiralled in circles when the star pattern was randomised (Dacke et al., *Current Biology* 23:4, 2013).

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#07091A` — deep night sky (ink / substrate)
  - `#F5ECD3` — Milky Way starlight cream
  - `#D4A94B` — brass sextant gold (compass / beetle highlight)
  - `#2A4A3C` — iridescent scarab green (beetle body)
  - `#9A9CB3` — cool stellar gray (grid, type secondary)
- **Mood:** cosmic, reverent, exacting
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A huge diagonal **Milky Way** band arcing across the upper two-thirds — scattered star field densifies inside it.
  - A **brass half-protractor** at the base, centred on a tiny side-view **beetle rolling a dung ball**. Degree marks tick every 15° along the galactic azimuth.
  - A **bearing line** drawing from the beetle straight up through the galactic plane, with a tick where it crosses — the composition's hook: *tiny insect, whole galaxy, one straight walk.*
  - Astronomical-plate type: FIG. 1 caption, celestial grid lines fading into the sky.
- **Motion:** one primary motion — the bearing line draws upward with a spring easing, snaps a tick onto the Milky Way, then the star field twinkles and the beetle's ball rolls forward a quarter turn. A 6-second loop.
