# The Glass Scalpel

**Pairing:** Obsidian as Surgeon

## The hook
A freshly fractured obsidian edge tapers to an average tip radius of about **3 nanometers** — roughly two orders of magnitude finer than the ~300 nm edge of a surgical-steel scalpel — and obsidian blades have been used in ophthalmic and reconstructive surgery precisely because their cleaner cuts leave less crush trauma at the incision margin (see Buck, *The Western Journal of Medicine*, 1982; Disa et al., *Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery*, 1993).

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#07070A` — obsidian black (background / negative space)
  - `#141319` — deep glass interior tone
  - `#2A2F3B` — cool graphite reflection
  - `#E9EEF3` — clinical highlight / cold surgical light
  - `#7A2E1F` — mahogany-obsidian iridescence accent (real streak colour in some flows)
- **Mood:** cold, precise, clinical, quietly menacing
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A single sculpted obsidian blade rendered as a tall vertical sliver crossing the frame at an angle, its conchoidal fracture ripples catching the surgical light. A thin luminous edge line traces one side.
  - A magnifier / call-out inset in the upper-right corner that zooms into the very tip of the edge and labels its ~3 nm radius against a schematic steel edge for scale — this is the composition that fuses "volcanic glass" with "surgeon."
  - A faint horizon line and instrument-tray grid behind the blade — the operating theatre without depicting one.
- **Motion:** a single specular light sweep travels slowly down the length of the blade on a soft spring, revealing the conchoidal texture and then settling at the tip; the scale call-out springs in a beat later. A calm ~5-second loop, no gimmicks.
