# The Beetle That Reads the Milky Way

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Celestial Navigator

## The hook
On moonless nights the African ball-rolling dung beetle *Scarabaeus satyrus* holds a straight bearing away from the dung pile by fixing its course on the diffuse light stripe of the Milky Way — the first insect ever shown to navigate by our galaxy, demonstrated in a planetarium by Dacke et al., *Current Biology* 23 (2013): 298–300.

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 x 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#0A0E1F` — moonless-sky ink (background)
  - `#151A30` — deep sky panel (chart body)
  - `#EDE6C9` — starlight cream (stars, title, ticks)
  - `#8677B8` — Milky Way lavender glow (galactic band)
  - `#C99A55` — warm ochre (dung ball, bearing line, seal)
- **Mood:** hushed, precise, cosmic yet artisanal
- **Key visual elements:**
  - Full-bleed night sky occupying the upper two-thirds — an arced, softly clumped Milky Way band sweeping diagonally across the frame.
  - A brass sextant-style astrolabe ring inset on the sky, with tick marks and a fine-ruled bearing line running from the beetle up through the galactic band.
  - Small silhouette of *S. satyrus* backing its dung ball along the bearing at the lower-right of the sky panel; the ball is the one saturated warm dot in an otherwise cool composition.
  - Strong left-aligned type block below the chart: uppercase ROLE tag, big italic display title, single-line hook.
- **Motion:** stars twinkle on a low-amplitude noise; the astrolabe ring rotates once by a few degrees on a spring easing to lock the bearing; a faint bearing beam pulses down from the Milky Way onto the beetle; the beetle inches forward along the line. 5-second loop.
