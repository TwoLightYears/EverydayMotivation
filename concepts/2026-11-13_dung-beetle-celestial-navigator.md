# The Beetle Who Steers by the Galaxy

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Celestial Navigator

## The hook
On moonless African nights, the South African dung beetle *Scarabaeus satyrus* climbs atop its ball of dung, does a brief "orientation dance," and rolls off in a straight line by using the dim band of the Milky Way as a compass — the first non-human animal shown to navigate by the galaxy itself (Dacke et al., *Current Biology* 23, 298–300, 2013). In planetarium trials, beetles orient reliably when only the galactic band is shown and lose the bearing when it is hidden.

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#070912` — night-sky ink
  - `#101627` — deeper horizon
  - `#F3E7C4` — Milky Way cream (dust-lane glow)
  - `#C4D4F4` — star-field pale blue
  - `#D4A54C` — brass of a navigator's instrument / azimuth ring
- **Mood:** contemplative, cosmic, exacting
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A full-bleed starfield with the Milky Way arcing diagonally across the upper two-thirds of the drafting frame.
  - A navigator's brass azimuth ring centered on the dung ball at lower-middle, with hairlines projecting outward across the sky to the galactic axis.
  - A small, silhouetted beetle poised atop its sphere — the mark being charted.
  - Faint ruled graticule & degree marks: this is a star chart, not just a landscape.
- **Motion:** stars fade in → Milky Way band washes along its axis → azimuth rays sweep out from the ball → the brass bearing indicator rotates and locks onto the galactic plane with a spring settle; subtle starlight twinkle loops across ~5 seconds.
