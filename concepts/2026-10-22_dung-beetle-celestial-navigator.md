# The Celestial Navigator

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Celestial Navigator

## The hook
The nocturnal African dung beetle *Scarabaeus satyrus* steers a straight escape line from a dung pile by orienting to the polarized-light stripe of the Milky Way itself — the first non-human animal confirmed to navigate by the galaxy, demonstrated when beetles kept their heading under a star-filled planetarium sky but lost it when only individual stars were shown (Dacke et al., *Current Biology* 23(4): 298–300, 2013).

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 x 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#070A1C` — deep night ink (field / negative space)
  - `#1B2749` — midnight navigation sky (gradient top of sky)
  - `#E6DFC9` — Milky Way starlight band (warm dust-cream)
  - `#C9A66B` — compass gold / dung-ball highlight
  - `#4A3B22` — scarab body / dung umber
- **Mood:** nocturnal, exacting, quietly cosmic — a field astronomer's chart with a very small animal at its centre
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A tall, slightly curved Milky Way band drawn as drifting dust and pinpoint stars across the upper two-thirds of the frame.
  - A silhouetted dung beetle perched on top of its small, textured dung ball at the bottom-centre — the "orientation dance" posture the paper actually describes.
  - A thin golden sightline rising from the beetle, locked to the Milky Way's axis, with faint bearing tick marks on either side (a planetarium-style azimuth reticle).
  - A compact lower-left title lockup: display italic title, uppercase ROLE tag, one-line hook; the Milky Way does the hero work above it, nothing centred by default.
- **Motion:** the sky's bearing reticle makes one slow spring-eased sweep around the beetle while the Milky Way holds — dramatising the finding that it is the galactic stripe, not individual stars, that fixes the beetle's heading. Stars twinkle softly on a 5-second loop.
