# The Beetle Who Steers By The Galaxy

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Celestial Navigator

## The hook
In a planetarium and moonless-savannah study, the African ball-rolling dung beetle *Scarabaeus satyrus* held perfectly straight paths using only the diffuse light of the Milky Way as its compass — the first invertebrate ever proven to navigate by our galaxy (Dacke, Baird, Byrne, Scholtz & Warrant, "Dung Beetles Use the Milky Way for Orientation," *Current Biology* 23: 298–300, 2013).

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#050812` — deep night ink (sky, negative space)
  - `#F3E6C4` — starlight cream (Milky Way core, bright stars)
  - `#8FA1C7` — cool galactic-dust blue (Milky Way haze, faint stars)
  - `#C1855B` — warm savannah / dung earth (horizon, beetle, ball)
  - `#E9B84A` — magnitude-star amber (bright anchor stars, bearing marks)
- **Mood:** hushed, cosmic, quietly heroic
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A high, arcing Milky Way band tilted across a starfield, painted as a dense cream ribbon fringed with dust-blue haze and scattered amber magnitudes.
  - A low, dark savannah horizon along the bottom third with the tiniest possible silhouette — a beetle pushing a spherical dung ball — walking a perfectly straight line.
  - A dotted bearing line rising from the beetle up into the sky, angled exactly parallel to the tilt of the Milky Way, arguing the pairing visually: its course *is* the galaxy's course.
- **Motion:** the starfield and Milky Way fade in first, the bearing line then draws itself upward along the galactic tilt, and the beetle rolls a short distance along its perfectly straight path while faint stars twinkle. Calm, ~5 s.
