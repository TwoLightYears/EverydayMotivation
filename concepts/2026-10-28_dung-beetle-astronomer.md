# The Beetle That Reads the Galaxy

**Pairing:** African Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Astronomer

## The hook

To get its dung ball away from the roll-stealing scrum at a dropping, the African dung beetle *Scarabaeus satyrus* needs to leave in a straight line — and on moonless nights it does so by orienting to the diffuse band of the Milky Way, the first non-human animal shown to use the galaxy as a compass (Dacke et al., *Current Biology* 23, 2013, 298–300: beetles kept their bearing under a planetarium sky that showed only the Milky Way, and lost it under a starless sky).

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080×1350 portrait.
- **Palette (drawn from the real Kalahari night and the matte-black beetle):**
  - `#06081A` — deep night-sky ink
  - `#141A36` — midnight atmosphere
  - `#E8DEC2` — Milky Way cream (the warm, yellowish galactic band as seen from a dark site)
  - `#C9A063` — starlight / dung-ball earth tone
  - `#0B0C10` — beetle carapace (near-black matte, with the faintest brown warmth)
- **Mood:** cosmic, patient, resolute.
- **Key visual elements:** a top-down navigator's chart. A broad diagonal arc of the Milky Way crosses the upper half of the composition; a thin bearing line passes through a single star on that arc, down through the small silhouette of the beetle and its dung ball at the lower-centre, like a sextant's sightline. Degree ticks on the inner frame reinforce the compass-rose reading. The composition's argument: this beetle isn't just walking — it is taking a bearing.
- **Motion:** the Milky Way band and star field fade and brighten with an eased spring, then the dashed bearing line draws itself from the beetle up through the chosen star — the single moment that dramatizes the hook.
