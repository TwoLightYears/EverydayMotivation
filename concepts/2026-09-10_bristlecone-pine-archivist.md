# The Tree That Files Millennia

**Pairing:** Great Basin Bristlecone Pine (*Pinus longaeva*) as Archivist

## The hook
A single bristlecone pine in California's White Mountains — nicknamed "Methuselah" — was 4,789 years old when a Schulman/Currey core was pulled from it in 1957, and overlapping ring sequences from living trees and preserved bristlecone deadwood have since been stitched into an unbroken **8,700-year** annual chronology that dendrochronologists use to calibrate radiocarbon dating (Ferguson & Graybill, LTRR; IntCal). Each ring is a single dated document, filed in order, still legible after five millennia.

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 x 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:** (5 hex values, drawn from Methuselah's real coloration — heartwood, exposed sun-bleached sapwood, high-alpine substrate)
  - `#0F0A07` — deep substrate / negative space
  - `#7B4E29` — dark heartwood / latewood ring
  - `#C69763` — warm earlywood / sapwood
  - `#EFE0B8` — parchment (paper index / labels)
  - `#8A8478` — weathered silver-gray (exposed dead wood)
- **Mood:** archival, patient, exacting
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A large tree-ring cross-section (the archive itself), centred in the upper two-thirds of the canvas, drawn as ~220 concentric annuli of varied earlywood/latewood tone.
  - The archivist's signature prop: dashed radial leader lines with year plates, filed like index cards to specific rings — 2832 BCE (pith), 776 BCE (First Olympiad), 1215 CE (Magna Carta), 1957 CE (cored & named).
  - The composition fuses the two: rings ARE the filing system; the leaders ARE the finding aid.
- **Motion:** rings reveal outward from the pith on a cubic-out easing, one dated leader clicking into place as the growing ring front passes its year; a single hairline "present-day" ring softly pulses when the reveal completes. ~5-second beat.
