# The Beetle That Steers by the Galaxy

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Celestial Navigator

## The hook
Dacke, Baird, Byrne, Scholtz & Warrant (*Current Biology* 23, 298–300, 2013) placed South African dung beetles under a planetarium sky and switched the star field on and off; with only the diffuse, polarized band of the Milky Way visible, *Scarabaeus satyrus* still rolled its ball in a perfectly straight line — the first animal proven to use the galaxy itself as a compass.

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 × 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#07091A` — deep midnight (sky base / negative space)
  - `#141A34` — twilight indigo (sky mid-tone, panel field)
  - `#F0E5B8` — Milky Way starlight (creamy warm-white)
  - `#8FA3D6` — cool star haze / polarization band highlight
  - `#C88A3E` — bronze accent (bearing markers, beetle chitin gleam)
- **Mood:** contemplative, cosmic, quietly precise
- **Key visual elements:**
  - The Milky Way rendered as a soft, elongated band of stars sweeping diagonally across the upper two-thirds of the frame.
  - A silhouetted *Scarabaeus satyrus* atop its dung ball on a hairline horizon, dead-centre lower quadrant — small enough that the galaxy dwarfs it.
  - A thin bronze bearing-line traced from the beetle up to a bright fix-point in the Milky Way, with tick-marked bearing gauge in the top-right — a navigator's plot on cosmic scale.
- **Motion:** stars fade in as a slow field; the Milky Way's polarized band drifts a few degrees; the bearing-line extends up to the fix-point on a single spring, and the bearing readout ticks to a settled azimuth. A calm 5-second loop.
