# The Beetle That Steers by the Galaxy

**Pairing:** Dung Beetle (*Scarabaeus satyrus*) as Celestial Navigator

## The hook
On moonless nights the South African dung beetle *Scarabaeus satyrus* rolls its ball in a dead-straight line by reading the faint polarization pattern of the Milky Way — the first non-human animal ever shown to use the galaxy itself as a compass (Dacke et al., *Current Biology* 23:4, 298–300, 2013).

## Visual brief

- **Dimensions:** 1080 x 1350 (portrait, social poster)
- **Palette:**
  - `#050914` — starless night ink (background substrate)
  - `#E9E4FF` — cool starlight highlight (Milky Way core, brightest stars)
  - `#7C7BB4` — dust-lane periwinkle (Milky Way midtones)
  - `#C9B681` — beetle carapace / dung ball warm ochre
  - `#8A8F99` — draftsman gray (annotation type, tickmarks)
- **Mood:** hushed, cosmological, precise
- **Key visual elements:**
  - A softly luminous arc of the Milky Way cutting diagonally across the upper two-thirds of the frame, rendered as stippled dust bands with a bright core.
  - A single tiny beetle silhouette pushing a warm ochre dung ball at the bottom horizon.
  - A dead-straight bearing line extends from the beetle backward across the "ground plane," annotated as a heading vector parallel to the galactic polarization axis — the hook made visible.
- **Motion:** the Milky Way's dust bands slowly resolve out of the dark; stars twinkle in place; the bearing line draws forward from the beetle along a spring easing, ending with a small compass rose fade-in that snaps to true galactic north. A calm ~5-second reveal, not a loop.
